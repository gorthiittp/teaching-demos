What the three squares are

The left square is the original image, a point in 64 dimensions. The middle one is that point projected onto the first M principal directions and mapped back, x̂ = x̄ + WMWM⊤(x − x̄). The right one is the difference between them, the part of the image that lives in the directions you discarded.

Slide M to zero. The reconstruction becomes the mean digit and every image in the set rebuilds to the same blur, because with no components there is nothing left that distinguishes one image from another. Every step up adds one number per image and one more direction to spend it on.

Where the error comes from

The average squared error over the training set is not an empirical quantity you have to measure — it is read off the spectrum directly:

average distortion = λM+1 + λM+2 + … + λ64

That is the grey tail to the right of the marker in the plot. The bars fall away quickly, which is the whole reason this works: a few directions carry most of the variation, and the remaining fifty-odd carry the rest between them.

The error shown for the individual digit is usually larger than the average, and sometimes much larger. An average over a dataset says nothing about the worst case, and an unusual digit — a crossed seven, a closed four — is exactly the kind of point whose information lives in the discarded directions.

When there are not enough images

Drag the training slider down to twelve. The spectrum stops after eleven bars and raising M past that point changes nothing, because a covariance matrix built from N points has rank at most N − 1. You cannot estimate more directions than your data can span, however many dimensions the measurements happen to have. With 64 pixels and 12 images you are in that regime; with 100,000 voxels and 200 subjects you are in it far more severely.

The subspace comes from the training set

Train on threes only, then rebuild an eight. The reconstruction tries to express the eight in a vocabulary of threes and produces something that is neither. PCA does not learn what digits look like in general; it learns the directions of greatest variation in whatever you showed it, and applies them to everything afterwards. This is also why the mean and the directions must come from the training split alone — a subspace fitted with the test data in it has already seen the answers.

Things to try

  • Find the smallest M at which you can still tell which digit it is. Compare that to the M the 95% rule picks. Legibility and reconstruction fidelity are different criteria, and the first is usually satisfied far sooner.
  • Watch the first three directions in the strip. They are not strokes or parts of digits; they are broad contrasts across the whole frame, and each image is a weighted sum of all of them.
  • Set the training images to 30 and press through the digits in the gallery. The reconstructions get visibly worse for the classes that happened to be under-represented.
  • Compare the average distortion with the single-image error as you raise M. Both fall, but not in step.
